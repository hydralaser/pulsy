Oinoe

a village on the Boeotian border, 5th century BC

Found the village

You are the leading man of Oinoe, a walled village on the border with Boeotia: the head of its richest household, in the big house at the top of the square. The other households look to you to say who works where.

Your standing word

How you answer each kind of petition without being asked every time. Petitions you answer this way still count for and against you as if you had answered them yourself.

Projects

Men with nothing else to do go where a house is paying for hands, at the usual day wage of a man in the fields: poorer houses first, and those on good terms with the payer. Set how many your own projects take on, and who pays.

    How it works
    • Households run their own lives: their men work their own fields when they need it (ploughing, sowing, reaping) and hire out when not, their women keep house, and every hand goes out at harvest. For anything more, cutting wood, building a house, clearing land, taking the flock, they ask your leave; their petitions appear on the map. Granting earns goodwill; refusing costs it, the more if they were in need; and letting a house cut wood to sell where others already do sets them against it. "Council" lets you answer whole kinds of petition in advance. You can still give orders yourself:
    • Work is given to households. Tap a household's men or women (several households at once if you like), then tap a place on the map: a field, the woods, the pasture. "To the fields, wherever needed" sends them wherever the fields need hands, their own land first; "Home" puts them to work at home. Households are listed richest first; tap a household's name to see its land, stores and people.
    • Everyone old enough goes: children from seven help at half a grown man's rate, the old do what they can. Nothing is closed to either sex; strength tells in the heavy work, and skill grows with practice.
    • They walk out at dawn and must be home by dark, so distant work loses hours to walking. Slow the clock right down (◀, or the minus key) to watch the work: ploughmen walking the furrows behind their oxen, reapers and hoers in a line going up and down the field, woodcutters dragging logs home or loading the ox cart, herders walking round the grazing flock, the women at the well and the washing place.
    • Barley is ploughed and sown in autumn, half the fields each year, and reaped in early summer. Firewood burns all year, most in winter. The flock gives milk in spring and summer, and needs herders or it strays.
    • Blue dots are men and boys, red women and girls.
    • Households last longer than people. The eldest son takes over when the head dies or grows old; brothers set up their own households once there is a house for them, each taking his share of the land. When a man wants a house of his own, his household's card says so: choose that household's men or women and "Build a house", with stone from the quarry and timber from the woods.
    • A household can also clear rough ground for a new field (choose its men or women, then "Clear new land"): it pays the wages of anyone else's people who work on it, and keeps the brushwood and stones. New land on the margins yields a little less than the old fields.
